### The angle, and the collision it had to survive

The obvious framing ("a week off makes you stronger because of fatigue dissipation")
**already exists twice on this site** — `deload-the-recovery-week-you-keep-skipping`
(the mechanism) and `after-the-deload-rebound` (why the rebound is *not* automatic,
built on the Coleman 2024 trial where deloaders came back worse).

Writing the obvious post would have contradicted a live page. So the post's job is to
**fence the word "deload"**, which had been stretched to cover two different
interventions:

| | Deload week | Week off on holiday |
|---|---|---|
| In the gym | yes | no |
| Frequency | kept | zero |
| Daily movement | normal | often much higher |
| **Practice of the movement** | **kept** | **lost** |

Both clear fatigue. They do nothing the same to skill. That distinction is the post.

## ⚠️ The defect caught by the gate — and why the gate was run before trusting the build

The first `hugo --minify` returned **exit 0** and a clean summary, but the page count
stayed at 314 EN / 336 ZH — the new post was **absent from `public/`**.

Cause: the post's frontmatter said `date: 2026-11-04T10:00:00+08:00` while the build ran
at **08:40 HKT**. Hugo treated it as a future-dated post and silently skipped it. Exit 0,
no warning.

Fixed by setting `date: 2026-11-04T07:00:00+08:00` (a real past timestamp). Page count
then moved to 315 EN / 339 ZH and both index.html files appeared.

⚠️ **Rule for future posts: never set `date:` to a time later than the current clock, and
never trust a Hugo exit code alone — compare the page count and confirm the built
`index.html` exists.** The `test_multilingual.py` gate passed *while the post was
missing*, because it only checks that whatever built is paired. It cannot see an
absent page. This is a new failure mode not in the skill; it should be added.

## Evidence base (all verified against primary sources before writing)

- **Coleman et al. 2024**, *PeerJ* 12:e16777, PMID 38274324 — 39 trained adults, 9 weeks,
  complete 1-week cessation vs continuous. No strength benefit from the break; continuous
  favoured on isometric + dynamic lower-body strength; no hypertrophy difference; break
  group reported increased soreness and decreased motivation. ⚠️ Funded by Renaissance
  Periodization, which had a role in design and manuscript prep — disclosed in the post.
- **Pritchard et al. 2018**, *J Strength Cond Res* 32(2):458–465 — 8 trained men, ~3.5 vs
  ~5.5 days cessation after 4-week blocks. Isometric bench press peak force and CMJ height
  **increased** significantly (ES 0.30 and 0.21). Attributed to decreased neuromuscular
  fatigue. This is the direct evidence for the post's central claim.
- **Ogasawara et al. 2011**, *Clin Physiol Funct Imaging* 31(5):399–404 — 3-week detraining
  produced no significant decrease in 1RM or MRI-measured triceps/pectoral CSA; retraining
  gains matched initial gains.
- **Ogasawara et al. 2013**, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 113(4):975–985 — 24 weeks, continuous vs
  periodic with 3-week breaks. Overall gains similar; periodic group gained **more** in the
  second retraining cycle. Detraining: 1RM −2.0% / −3.3%, MVC unchanged.
- **Knowles et al. 2018**, *J Sci Med Sport* 21(9):959–968 — systematic review, 17 studies.
  Sleep restriction reduced force in **multi-joint** but not single-joint movements.

⚠️ **Population limit stated in-post:** Coleman/Pritchard/Ogasawara cohorts were young and
two were small (n=8, n=9 per group). None tested men over 40. The post says so and says the
directions of effect are not measured magnitudes for a 45-year-old.

⚠️ **Personal-claim discipline:** the post uses only what Vincent said — a week in Tokyo,
no gym, bench up one level on return. It explicitly states he did **not** measure his steps
and that the mechanism is a hypothesis with good support on each half, not a measured
result about him. No invented details, no invented numbers.
