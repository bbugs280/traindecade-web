Ask the internet whether you should cut or bulk first, and you'll get two confident, contradictory answers delivered with equal authority.

One voice says *"you can't build muscle in a deficit, bulk first."* The other says *"you can't look good buried in fat, cut first."* Both are selling you the same underlying mistake: the idea that cutting and bulking are opposing philosophies rather than two phases of the same repeating cycle.

Once you see them as phases — not identities — the whole question collapses into something simpler: **what phase are you in, how long should it last, and what does "done" look like?**

## The one decision that actually matters

You don't pick "cut" or "bulk" in the abstract. You pick based on where you are *right now*:

- **Carrying more fat than you want? Cut.** If you can pinch real fat at the waist, a calorie deficit is your phase. Adding muscle while visibly overweight is the slowest possible path to looking different.
- **Lean enough, but small? Gain.** If you're relatively lean but under-muscled — the classic "skinny-fat" or just plain thin — a modest surplus is where the fastest visible change happens.
- **Both? You're a beginner or early intermediate.** If you haven't trained more than a year or two, you don't need to pick at all. Recomposition — losing fat and gaining muscle simultaneously — is real, and it's *easiest* exactly here, when the gap between where you are and where you could be is widest.

The trap isn't picking wrong. The trap is never picking — drifting between "sort of cutting" and "sort of bulking" and getting neither.

## How long each phase should last

The evidence points to asymmetric timelines: **phases of building are slow; phases of cutting are faster.** Muscle is built at a glacial pace — the realistic ceiling for a trained man is measured in a few kilograms of muscle *per year*. Fat, by contrast, can come off at a meaningful rate every single week.

So the structure most evidence-based coaches land on is roughly:

- **Gain phase: 4–6 months or longer.** Building muscle is slow, and a longer, modest surplus (a few hundred calories over maintenance) produces more muscle and less fat than a short, aggressive bulk. Rushing a bulk just makes you fat faster.
- **Cut phase: 6–12 weeks.** A deficit of 300–500 calories, sustained long enough to actually strip meaningful fat, but short enough that you're not white-knuckling through months of hunger and losing the muscle you built.
- **Repeat.** The cycle isn't a one-time decision. It's a rhythm you'll run for years — which is exactly the framing that separates a phase from a crash.

## The trap: cutting too hard

Here's where most men over 40 go wrong, and the evidence is unforgiving about it.

When you diet, you don't just lose fat — you lose *some* muscle and lean mass with it. The question is how much. The research is consistent: **resistance training plus adequate protein is what decides whether a cut strips fat or strips you.**

The clearest number: dieting alone turns about **24%** of lost weight into lean mass. Adding resistance training cuts that to **~11%**. And in a famous study (Longland et al.), men in a steep **40% calorie deficit** who ate high protein (~2.4 g/kg) and trained still managed to *gain* muscle while losing fat. The deficit didn't cost them their muscle — the lack of protein and training would have.

The takeaway is blunt: **the crash cut is how you end up smaller *and* softer — the exact opposite of the goal.** A steeper deficit helps you reach "done" faster, but "done" includes muscle you spent months building if you skip the training and protein that protect it.

## How to know you're "done" with a phase

One of the quiet reasons people fail is they never define the endpoint. A phase without an exit condition isn't a phase — it's just a new normal you'll drift in forever.

- **Cut is done when** you can see the definition you were after, *or* when the deficit is no longer sustainable without losing training performance and lean mass. That second signal matters more than the mirror: when your lifts are dropping and you're starving, you're past the point of productive cutting.
- **Gain is done when** you've either hit a muscle goal you can measure (a lift, a measurement) or the fat you're accruing alongside the muscle has reached the point where more bulking isn't worth it anymore.
- **Then switch.** Every phase ends in the next one. That's the whole game.

## The decade framing

Here's the part the six-week-challenge crowd never tells you: **you will run this cycle many times.** Cut, gain, cut, gain — not once, but for as long as you train.

The man who treats each phase as a six-week sprint burns out, rebounds, and starts over, perpetually. The man who treats them as phases of a decade-long project looks different every single year — because each cycle leaves a little more muscle behind and takes a little more fat off, permanently.

You don't need to nail your first cut. You need to keep running the cycle.

A decade is coming either way. Spend it as a man who knows which phase he's in.
