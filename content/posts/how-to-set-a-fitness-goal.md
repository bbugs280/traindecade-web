Before you pick a program, a race, or a calorie target, there's a quieter problem standing in the way: **you don't actually have a goal.**

Not a real one. You have a *wish* — "get in shape," "lose weight," "get shredded for summer" — and wishes don't steer anything. They don't tell you what to do on a Tuesday, they don't survive a bad week, and they certainly don't survive ten years.

Here's how to replace the wish with a goal that actually lasts.

## Why most goals fail before you start

Most fitness goals are dead on arrival, and it's rarely laziness. It's that they're built wrong:

**They're outcomes you can't control.** "Lose 10 kg" depends on your body's response to a deficit, not your effort. You can train and eat perfectly and still miss the number — and when the number is the goal, you feel like *you* failed rather than the forecast.

**They're borrowed.** "Look like [influencer]" or "keep up with the 25-year-olds at the gym" is someone else's metric, with someone else's genetics, timeline, and priorities underneath it. You're aiming at a photo, not a version of *your* life.

**They're too vague to steer by.** "Get in shape" gives you nothing to do at 6 a.m. A useful goal is one that answers the question *what do I do next?* If it can't answer that, it isn't a goal — it's a mood.

The before-and-after industry wants you to keep the wish, because a wish keeps failing, and a failing customer keeps coming back for the next six-week fix. A real goal is something they can't sell you — which is exactly why it works.

## Outcome goals vs. process goals

The single most useful distinction in goal-setting is between the result you *can't* control and the behaviour you *can*.

- **Outcome goal:** "weigh 82 kg by March."
- **Process goal:** "train three times a week for six months, and eat at maintenance with enough protein."

Outcome goals are fine as a *direction*. The mistake is making them the thing you judge yourself against, because the outcome is never fully yours. The process is. When you set a goal around the behaviour — show up, lift, eat, sleep — you can win *this week* regardless of what the scale says.

That's the goal that actually lasts: it doesn't expire when you miss a target. You can always re-commit to the process tomorrow, which is the whole point of a decade-scale project.

## Ask the decade-size question

Here's the question that cuts through all of it, borrowed from the [long-game view](/posts/why-a-decade-not-a-6-week-challenge/):

**What do you want to still be able to do at 55? At 65?**

Not "what do you want to weigh." Not "what do you want to look like." *Do.* Pick up your kid. Carry the groceries and the suitcase. Hike the trail you've been putting off. Ski without wrecking your knees. Stand up off the floor without using your hands. Get out of a chair and climb stairs without thinking about it.

That's the real goal. Everything else — the race, the recomp, the PR — is a *milestone* on the way to it, not the destination itself. And it's the goal that gives the decade a shape, because it's not satisfied by a single before-and-after. It's satisfied by still showing up, year after year.

## How to know it's the right goal

Run any candidate goal through three filters:

**Can you measure it?** "Feel better" is wonderful, but it can't tell you if this month worked. A goal needs a number, a date, or a clear "yes, I did that" — even if the measure is as simple as "three sessions this week."

**Is it actually yours?** If the goal survives a quiet week alone with no one watching, it's probably yours. If it only matters when you're posting about it, it might be borrowed.

**Does it survive a bad month?** The real test. A goal that collapses the first time you skip a week wasn't a goal — it was a mood with a deadline. The right one bends without breaking, because it's built on process, not outcome.

If a goal passes all three, it's worth a decade. If it fails one, fix that part before you invest the years.

## Then the race makes sense

This is where something like [Hyrox](/posts/will-hyrox-help-you-reach-your-goal/) finally clicks into place. A race isn't the goal — but once you *have* a goal, a race becomes a brilliant **milestone**: a fixed date that turns "someday" into a twelve-week countdown, a measurable checkpoint, and a reason to show up on the days the decade feels far away.

The order matters, and most people get it backwards. They sign up for the race *first*, treat it as the goal, and wonder why the motivation evaporates the week after. The people who make it stick do it the other way around: **goal first, then milestones.** The race serves the goal. It never replaces it.

Set the decade-size goal. Then point the race at it, and let the clock, the reps, and the years do the rest.

## Sources

- **Outcome vs. process goals** — A well-established distinction in motivation and behaviour-change research: process (behavioural) goals are within the individual's control and are more effective at sustaining adherence than pure outcome goals, which depend on factors outside direct control. (Foundational in self-regulation and goal-setting literature.)
- **Self-determination theory and goal internalisation** — Intrinsic or self-endorsed goals (autonomous motivation) predict sustained behaviour far better than externally imposed or appearance-driven goals; "borrowed" goals fail because they lack internal ownership. (Deci & Ryan.)
- **Specific, measurable goals and performance** — Specific, measurable goals outperform vague "do your best" goals across domains; specificity converts intention into steerable action. (Classic goal-setting research, Locke & Latham.)
- **Strength and function as predictors of healthy ageing** — Grip strength, chair-stand ability, and lower-body function in middle age are strong predictors of independence and mortality risk decades later; framing the decade goal around *function* (what you can still do) rather than *appearance* is evidence-aligned. (Multiple longitudinal cohort studies, e.g. grip-strength and all-cause mortality meta-analyses.)
