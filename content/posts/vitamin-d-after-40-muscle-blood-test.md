# Vitamin D After 40: The Muscle Number Your Blood Test Hides

There's a number hiding on the blood panel most men over 40 skip reading. It's not cholesterol, not HbA1c, not testosterone. It's **vitamin D** — and if it's low, it's quietly doing more damage to your muscle than you think.

Here's the catch, and it's the entire point of this post: vitamin D is **not a supplement that makes you stronger**. It's a *deficiency marker*. Below a threshold it's associated with weaker muscles and worse physical function. Above that threshold, taking more does almost nothing. The supplement industry wants you to forget that distinction, because "take this to get jacked" sells better than "check this one number so you know you're not deficient."

## The number that matters

The threshold is **30 nmol/L** (that's 25-hydroxyvitamin D, the form your blood test measures). Below it, you're *deficient*. And in a large study of over 4,000 adults aged 60+ in England (the English Longitudinal Study of Aging), deficiency showed up in the body's strength in a measurable way:

- Adults with vitamin D below 30 nmol/L had **1.44× the odds** of low handgrip strength.
- They had **1.65× the odds** of poor physical performance on a standard battery of chair-stands, balance, and walking tests.
- The lowest-vitamin-D group had **40% impaired muscle strength** vs 22% in the well-supplied group.

Grip strength, for what it's worth, is one of the best single predictors of overall health in older adults — it's a whole-body muscle proxy, not just a hand thing. So a blood marker that tracks with grip strength is a blood marker that tracks with the thing you're actually trying to preserve.

All of this is *association*, not proof of cause — observational, cross-sectional, one snapshot. But the direction is consistent across studies, and it points at the same conclusion: **being deficient is the problem, not being unsupplemented.**

## The supplement trap (read this before you buy the bottle)

Here's where the honest nuance lives, and it's the part the marketing leaves out.

Randomized trials — the gold standard that actually tests "does taking more vitamin D make you stronger?" — mostly find **no benefit for people who aren't deficient.** Multiple meta-analyses of vitamin D supplementation on muscle strength, power, and physical performance come back null for the general population. The study authors put it bluntly: supplementation "does not improve muscle power, strength, and physical performance in older adults."

So the lever isn't "take vitamin D to build muscle." It's:

```
Deficient (<30 nmol/L)  →  fixing the deficiency matters, for muscle AND bone
Sufficient (>50 nmol/L) →  taking more is just expensive urine
```

The supplement that does reliably help muscle — the one with the actual meta-analysis behind it — is **creatine**, not vitamin D. (We broke that one down [here](/posts/creatine-after-40-do-you-actually-need-it/).) Vitamin D is the *status check*, creatine is the *ergogenic aid*. People conflate the two because they both sit on the "man over 40" supplement shelf, but they do completely different jobs.

## Who actually needs to care

Vitamin D deficiency is genuinely common — and some of the biggest risk factors are the most mundane:

- **Working indoors all day** (office, gym-at-night) — the classic 40-something desk-worker profile.
- **Living at high latitude** or low-winter-sun climates.
- **Darker skin** (melanin blocks UV synthesis) — under-discussed and often missed.
- **Not eating much fatty fish** (salmon, mackerel, sardines are the main food sources).
- **Being heavier** — vitamin D is fat-soluble and gets sequestered in adipose tissue.

If you tick several of those, you're exactly the person whose blood panel deserves a second look — not because a capsule will make you stronger, but because an *undetected deficiency* will quietly hold you back in ways you can't feel until it's already cost you.

## The 30-second version

1. **Get the number.** Ask for 25-hydroxyvitamin D on your next blood draw. Don't guess — the "I take a multivitamin, I'm fine" assumption is how deficiency hides.
2. **Only the deficient need to act.** Below 30 nmol/L → fix it (high-dose correction, then a maintenance dose, usually 800–2000 IU/day depending on who you ask). Above 50 → you're fine, save your money.
3. **Don't expect a strength jump.** Correcting a deficiency protects function; it doesn't build muscle. That's what [training](/posts/strength-training-after-40-how-to-start/) and [protein](/posts/how-much-protein-do-you-really-need/) are for.
4. **Treat sun + food first.** 10–20 minutes of arm-and-leg sun a few times a week, plus fatty fish, does more for the average person than a pill — and it's free.

Vitamin D is the number your blood test already has but nobody circled. It won't make you jacked. It will tell you whether your body is running with one tank half-empty — and at 40+, that's information worth having.

- **Rosendahl-Riise H, et al. "Vitamin D supplementation and its influence on muscle strength and mobility in community-dwelling older persons: a systematic review and meta-analysis."** — supplementation showed no consistent improvement in muscle strength/mobility without calcium; benefit concentrated in correction of true deficiency.
- **American Journal of Clinical Nutrition — vitamin D supplementation trials round-up.** — recent trials show supplementation does not improve muscle power/strength/physical performance in non-deficient older adults; the 30 nmol/L deficiency cut-off is where correction matters.
- **Sharifian G, et al. (2025), *European Review of Aging and Physical Activity* — creatine meta-analysis.** — the contrast case: creatine *does* improve 1RM strength in older adults (P=0.001), unlike vitamin D, which is a status marker. Cited to make the "two different jobs" point clear.
