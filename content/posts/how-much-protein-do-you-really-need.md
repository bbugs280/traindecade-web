Ask ten people how much protein you need and you'll get ten numbers, each delivered with total confidence. On one side: the "one gram per pound" crowd, quoting gym-locker geometry. On the other: the "bro-science is a myth" crowd, pointing at an official guideline so conservative it was designed for sedentary survival, not muscle.

Both are wrong in a way that's actually convenient for you to hear, because the real answer is a single number that the research keeps landing on — and it's less than the bros insist and far more than the skeptics admit.

**The evidence-backed dose for building muscle is about 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day.** For a man at 80 kg (176 lb), that's roughly 128 g a day. Past that point, extra protein stops meaningfully adding muscle and just becomes expensive calories. Below it — especially as you get older — you're leaving gains on the table.

## Why the two competing numbers are both wrong

The "one gram per pound" rule is gym-locker folklore, not science. It was born from a convenient rounding, repeated so often it calcified into certainty. The actual research plateau — the dose beyond which adding protein doesn't build more muscle — lands around **1.6 g/kg/day** (or, said the American way, about **0.73 g per pound**). The 1g-per-pound crowd is recommending roughly 35% more protein than the evidence says does anything.

That's not harmless. Excess protein isn't toxic, but it crowds out the foods that matter — and it makes a supposedly simple plan feel like a chore, which is how people quit.

The other extreme — the official RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day — is a genuine number with a genuine purpose: it's the minimum to avoid deficiency in a *sedentary* adult. It was never meant to be a target for someone trying to build or hold muscle. Citing it as "science says you're fine" is how people convince themselves that their shrinking muscle mass is just age. It's a floor, not a ceiling — and for the over-40 trainer, it's a lowball that costs you muscle every year you follow it.

## Why the number matters *more* after 40

Here's the thread that ties this to every other decade-scale argument you'll read on this site: **protein gets more important with age, not less.**

Two mechanisms drive it. First, aging blunts the anabolic response — the same protein dose triggers less muscle synthesis in a 55-year-old than a 25-year-old. Second, many people eat *less* protein as they age, precisely when they need more. The result is the slow, almost imperceptible creep of sarcopenia: the loss of muscle that drags strength, balance, and bone health down with it over decades.

The fix is boring and specific: aim for the **1.6 g/kg/day** end of the range, distributed across your meals (the synthetic response is dose-sensitive — a bolus of 0.4 g/kg per meal, roughly 25–40 g, reliably maximizes it). The over-40 lifter who does this closes most of the "age gap" in muscle-building.

## What that looks like on a plate

Forget the supplement-store theatrics. The math is simpler than the industry wants you to think:

- **80 kg man → ~128 g/day**, or ~40 g across three meals (plus a little extra).
- That's a palm of chicken (~30 g), two eggs (~12 g), a scoop of whey or a Greek yogurt (~20–25 g), a tin of tuna (~25 g), a serving of beef (~35 g). You don't need a spreadsheet — you need roughly a palm-sized protein source per meal, three times a day.

Whole foods beat powders — not because powders are bad, but because food carries the micronutrients, satiety, and habit you're actually building a decade on. A scoop of whey is a fine gap-filler, not a strategy.

## The decade framing

Nobody's muscle mass collapses because they aimed 10 g under their protein target one Tuesday. The damage is the *decades* spent at the RDA, thinking 0.8 g/kg was "science-approved" while muscle quietly receded.

A man at 45 who eats 1.6 g/kg and trains two to three times a week isn't doing anything dramatic. He's just removing the single most fixable reason men his age lose muscle — and letting the training actually work.

Protein isn't magic. It's the raw material. Get the dose right and the boring consistency actually compounds; get it wrong for years and no amount of effort in the gym fully lands.

## Sources

- **Morton et al. (2018)** — "A systematic review, meta-analysis and meta-regression of the effect of protein supplementation on resistance training-induced gains in muscle mass and strength in healthy adults." Protein intakes beyond ~1.6 g/kg/day produced no further benefit.
- **Bagheri et al.** — Untrained older adults supplementing toward ~1.6 g/kg/day gained significantly more lean mass and strength over 8 weeks than those at ~0.8 g/kg/day — evidence the higher dose matters specifically for older trainees.
- **Anabolic resistance with age** — Older adults show a blunted muscle-protein-synthetic response per unit of protein, which is partially overcome by higher per-meal doses (~0.4 g/kg, or ~25–40 g) and higher total daily intake.
- **RDA context** — The 0.8 g/kg/day Recommended Dietary Allowance is defined as the minimum to meet needs in 97–98% of *sedentary* adults, not a target for muscle maintenance or gain.
