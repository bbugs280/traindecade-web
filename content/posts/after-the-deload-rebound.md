You took the week. You dropped the volume, cut the load, and left reps in the tank like you were supposed to. And now you're standing in the gym on Monday, slightly nervous, waiting to feel the thing everyone promised.

The fresh legs. The easy PR. The "I forgot how good this feels."

Sometimes it's there. Sometimes it isn't — and if it isn't, most lifters draw the wrong conclusion. They assume the deload was wasted, or that they're getting old, or that their recovery is broken. What they rarely consider is that **the rebound was never automatic in the first place.** It has to be earned on the way *back*, not just on the way down.

## The promise, and where it comes from

The deload has a real mechanism behind it. Hard training builds adaptation and fatigue at the same time, and fatigue is faster to feel than adaptation is to express. Back off, let the fatigue clear, and the strength that was already banked becomes visible. That's the logic, and it's sound.

The problem is what that logic implies if you only read the first half. If fatigue is masking strength, then removing fatigue should reveal strength — automatically, on a schedule, like a bank withdrawal.

That's the part the evidence doesn't support.

## The study that complicates the story

A 2024 trial in *PeerJ* (Coleman and colleagues) tested this directly, and it's the first study to actually measure whether a deload potentiates later gains rather than just assuming it. Thirty-nine resistance-trained men and women ran a 9-week program at high volume — around 90 weekly sets, sets taken to volitional failure.

Half took a full week off in the middle. Half trained straight through.

The result, on the outcomes that matter most to a lifter:

- **Strength did not improve from deloading. It went backwards.** The continuous group gained more on both 1RM squat (posterior probability 85% favouring continuous training) and isometric knee extension strength (92% favouring continuous training).
- **Hypertrophy was a wash.** Both groups grew muscle similarly. No advantage either way.
- **Power and local endurance:** no meaningful difference.
- **And the subjective result is the interesting one.** The deload group reported *increased* muscle soreness and *decreased* motivation to train. Several participants described feeling lethargic and "out of practice" on return — the opposite of refreshed.

That last point deserves a pause. The entire premise of the deload is that you come back better. In this trial, the group that deloaded came back feeling *worse.* Not injured, not detrained — just flat, and not particularly motivated.

**The honest reading: a week off is not a treatment. It's a variable.** What it does depends on how it's used, and "one week, then straight back to normal" is exactly the pattern this study tested — and it underperformed.

## The two failure modes nobody names

Put that alongside the largest survey of real deload practice — 246 competitive strength and physique athletes, published in *Sports Medicine Open* (Rogerson and colleagues, 2024) — and a cleaner picture forms. That survey asked athletes what made a deload *fail* to achieve its purpose:

```
Trained too heavy               50.4%
Volume was too high             44.7%
The deload was too short        32.5%
Fatigue was too high            32.5%
```

Read the first two together and you get the classic error: reducing weight while keeping hard sets numerous. That's the one everyone talks about.

But look at the third. **A third of athletes said the deload was too short** — and this is where the studies connect. The Coleman trial used complete cessation, one week, then back to full training. The survey respondents who failed were often doing something similar: too little of a real recovery, followed by too abrupt a return.

The failure isn't in the reduction. It's in the **reversal.**

## What "coming back" actually requires

If the rebound isn't automatic, what produces it? The evidence points at three things, and none of them are a readiness score.

**1. Reduce, don't stop.** The Coleman trial's deload was complete abstinence, and the authors themselves flag it as a likely weakness: *"Perhaps a period of reduced training volume and intensity, but not complete cessation, would allow for the dissipation of fatigue without bringing about a feeling of lethargy upon return."* That matches the practice in the survey, where athletes kept the same exercises and frequency and only cut load and sets. **Keep training. Train lighter.** The habit and the movement pattern are part of what you're preserving.

**2. Stop judging recovery by feel alone.** This is where lifters get into trouble most often, because "how I feel" is a genuinely unreliable instrument. The HRV literature is blunt about it: resting HRV can be insensitive to overtraining, and it doesn't track neuromuscular readiness well. A narrative review on HRV monitoring puts it directly — *"despite a lack of direct associations between HRV and neuromuscular capabilities, HRV monitoring may improve training program design by prescribing adequate recovery."*

Translated: HRV is useful for spotting *trends over weeks*, and close to useless for deciding whether you're ready *today*. If your watch says "recovery 94%" and your warm-up says the bar feels heavy, believe the bar.

**3. Establish a baseline before you need it.** If you want any physiological signal to mean anything, it has to be compared against your own normal — and the guidance is specific: record consistently for **at least 1–2 weeks** before you use it to make any training decision. A single morning reading tells you nothing. And record it the same way every time, because morning and overnight HRV measure different things.

For a man over 40, this matters more, not less. Recovery runs on a slower clock with age, which means the *return* is where the over-40 lifter most reliably goes wrong. A 25-year-old can absorb an abrupt re-entry. A 45-year-old usually can't, and the bill arrives as a nagging joint or a stalled lift three weeks later.

## The re-entry, in one block

```
COMING BACK AFTER A DELOAD WEEK:

Week 1 back   →  ~80-85% of your pre-deload loads
                 Full-ish volume, but no sets to failure
                 Judge by the warm-up, not by motivation
Week 2 back   →  return to normal loads
                 If the bar still feels heavy, repeat week 1.
                 Two weeks at 85% is not lost time.

DO NOT:
  ✗  Jump straight back to your last working weights
  ✗  Chase a PR in your first week back
  ✗  Treat a number on a watch as clearance to train heavy
```

The deload is the easy half. The re-entry is the half that determines whether any of it worked.

## What this means if you're over 40

If you came off a deload week and the rebound didn't arrive, you probably didn't lose anything. You likely returned too fast, or you measured your readiness with an instrument that can't measure it.

The fix is unglamorous. Train lighter rather than stopping. Come back gradually rather than all at once. Trust the warm-up over the wearable. And give the signal a baseline before you ask it a question.

None of that produces a satisfying clang either — which is why, like the deload itself, most people skip it and then wonder why the week didn't deliver.

The deload clears the fatigue. [What you do in the fortnight after it](/posts/deload-the-recovery-week-you-keep-skipping/) is what decides whether the strength underneath actually shows up.
