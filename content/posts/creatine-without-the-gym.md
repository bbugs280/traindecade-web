A study published in August 2026 put creatine in front of a question the supplement industry has never been able to answer honestly: **does it do anything for a person who is not training at all?**

The short answer is yes, a little, and the size of the "little" is the entire story. Adults aged 45 to 65 who took 10 grams of creatine monohydrate daily for twelve weeks gained roughly **2.4 pounds of lean tissue without following any exercise programme**. They also got stronger on leg press and bench press. That was against placebo, so it is not a "they felt better" result.

Which raises the obvious question, and it is not the one the supplement aisle will sell you.

## What the study actually did

Sixty-four healthy adults, aged 45 to 65, twelve weeks. Participants first chose whether to join a structured programme of resistance and aerobic training three times a week alongside a modest calorie deficit, or to stay in a non-exercise group. They were then randomly assigned to 10 g of creatine monohydrate a day or a placebo.

Four groups, in other words, and this design is the reason the paper is worth reading:

```
                        creatine          placebo
exercising             + training        training only
not exercising         creatine only     placebo only
```

Most creatine trials compare "creatine + training" against "training". That design can only ever tell you creatine amplifies work. It cannot tell you whether creatine does anything on its own, because everyone in the trial is already training. This one put a real "nothing else" arm in the room.

## What it found, in plain numbers

**The non-exercisers gained lean tissue.** About 2.4 pounds over twelve weeks on creatine, against little change on placebo. They also improved leg press and bench press strength. Both matter, and the strength result is the more interesting of the two, because strength is what predicts whether you can still get off the floor at seventy.

**The exercisers on a deficit did better on creatine than without it.** Nearly three pounds of lean tissue gained while losing fat mass. The authors state this is the first trial to show that adding creatine to an exercise-plus-diet programme produces a greater drop in body-fat percentage than exercise and diet alone. That is a specific, testable claim, and it is the strongest single result in the paper.

**Creatine did nothing for aerobic capacity.** No improvement in aerobic fitness in either group. This is not a disappointment, it is a confirmation: creatine fuels short, hard efforts. If your goal is a faster 10k, this study gives you nothing. That null result is what makes the rest of the paper credible. A trial that finds a benefit everywhere it looks is usually measuring noise.

## The four things this does not prove

This is where a supplement company's blog post would stop. Here is the rest of it.

**Twelve weeks is not a decade.** Twelve weeks tells you the direction and roughly the size of an early effect. It cannot tell you whether that lean tissue keeps accumulating, plateaus at week twenty, or reverses when you stop. Age-related muscle loss is a thirty-year problem; a twelve-week window is a photograph of the first frame.

**"Lean tissue" on a body-composition scan is not the same as muscle you can use.** The measurement in this study was body composition, and body composition methods have known limitations. The strength improvements are the harder evidence, because a leg press number is not an estimate.

**Sixty-four people, one population.** Healthy adults, 45 to 65, one trial, one research group. The group's lead author has a long-standing advisory relationship with creatine manufacturers, and the paper's funding came through a foundation rather than a supplement company — which is better than the alternative, and still not independence. Read the result as "this is what one well-designed trial found," not "this is settled."

**It does not say creatine replaces training.** This is the sentence the marketing will delete. Every group that trained beat every group that did not. Creatine without training produced about 2.4 pounds of lean tissue over three months; creatine with training produced that plus strength, plus aerobic capacity, plus the fat-loss effect. The non-exercise arm is the *floor*, not the goal.

## So what does this actually change for a man over 40?

Three things, and none of them are "buy more creatine."

**It gives you something to say to the man who isn't training yet.** The standard advice — "creatine works if you train" — is useless to someone who does not train, and most men over 45 who ask about creatine are in exactly that position. This study says the honest answer is now: it does something small on its own, and the size of that something is roughly two pounds of lean tissue in three months. That is not nothing at forty-five. It is also not a reason to skip the gym.

**It reorders the priority, it does not remove one.** If you have creatine and no training, you are getting the smaller half of the benefit. If you have training and no creatine, you are getting most of it. The cheap supplement is not the expensive one's substitute.

**It lowers the excuse, not the bar.** "I'll start creatine when I start training" is now a slightly weaker position than it was in July. It is a five-gram-a-day habit that costs very little and does not require you to have a programme yet. Take it while you build the habit of training.

## The decade framing

Every post on this site about supplements makes the same move: separate what the evidence shows from what the seller says it shows. This study is a good test case, because the honest reading is genuinely unglamorous.

```text
what the headline will say          what the paper says
"creatine builds muscle            about 2.4 lb lean tissue in 12 weeks
 without the gym"                   in 45-65 year olds, vs placebo
"no exercise needed"               no exercise needed for a SMALL effect,
                                    less than a third of the training effect
"proven in older adults"           one trial, n=64, one age band
```

The useful version of this story is not the one that makes creatine sound like a workout substitute. It is the one that tells a fifty-two-year-old who has not started yet that the smallest possible action — five grams, no gym required — has a real, measured effect, and that the bigger effect is still sitting there waiting for him whenever he is ready to train.

That is the whole argument this site keeps making. The villain is the six-week promise. The hero is the boring lever that compounds for years. Creatine just moved slightly further onto the right side of that line, and the honest size of the move is about two pounds.

If you already train, none of this changes what you should do — three to five grams a day, every day, and keep lifting. What changed is the advice you give the man who hasn't started. ## Sources

- **Kreider RB, et al. (2026)** — "Effects of creatine supplementation with and without exercise and diet intervention on body composition, cognitive function, and markers of health in middle-aged and older adults." *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 11 August 2026. DOI: 10.1080/15502783.2026.2716273. Sixty-four healthy adults aged 45–65; 10 g/day creatine monohydrate or placebo for 12 weeks, with or without a structured resistance plus aerobic programme and modest calorie deficit. Non-exercise + creatine group gained ~2.4 lb lean tissue and improved leg and bench press strength versus placebo. Exercise + diet + creatine group gained ~3 lb lean tissue while reducing fat mass; authors report this as the first study to show creatine plus exercise and diet produces a greater reduction in body-fat percentage than exercise and diet alone. No benefit to aerobic capacity. Funded through the WoodNext Foundation via the Texas A&M Foundation.
- **Disclosure** — the senior author holds a scientific advisory role with commercial creatine interests. Stated here because it is material to how much weight this single trial should carry.
- **Devries MC, Phillips SM (2014)** — "Creatine supplementation during resistance training in older adults — a meta-analysis." Creatine plus resistance training improved fat-free mass and upper/lower-body strength versus training alone. Cited here as the reason the training effect should be treated as larger and better established than the non-training effect.
- **Sharifian G, et al. (2025)** — "Impact of creatine supplementation and exercise training in older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis." *Eur Rev Aging Phys Act* 22:17. Twenty RCTs, 1,093 adults aged 55+, creatine plus exercise. Significant 1-RM strength gains versus placebo (mean difference 2.12 kg, P=0.001); bone mineral density not significantly improved (P=0.557), and the body-fat effect did not survive sensitivity analysis.
- **Texas A&M University (18 September 2026)** — "Study finds creatine's benefits extend beyond the gym." University release describing the same trial; used to cross-check the figures above against the institution's own summary rather than press coverage.
