"Give it 48 hours." It's the most repeated number in strength training — repeated in gyms, on forums, in programmes, and by coaches who mean well. Heavy lifting "fries your CNS," and the CNS apparently needs two days to reboot.

The advice lands on the right answer through the wrong mechanism. Which matters, because if you recover on a timeline you can actually measure, you recover differently.

This post is the third in a short series on central fatigue. The [primer on what CNS fatigue is and how HRV catches it](/posts/your-nervous-system-is-fried/) gives you the framework. [The myth put on trial](/posts/does-heavy-lifting-fry-your-cns/) handles whether heavy lifting "fries" anything at all. This one answers the question both of those raise but don't settle: **how long does it actually take to recover, hour by hour?**

## The study that measured the timeline instead of guessing

Most of the "48 hours" claim rests on assumption rather than measurement. So in 2018 a group at Northumbria University did the obvious thing — they measured it.

Thomas and colleagues took ten trained male athletes and put them through three separate maximal sessions, a week apart:

- **Strength:** 10 × 5 back squats at 80% of 1RM
- **Jump:** 10 × 5 jump squats
- **Sprint:** 15 × 30 m maximal sprints

Then they tested neuromuscular function at 24, 48, and 72 hours. Not a questionnaire. **Electrical stimulation of the femoral nerve** to probe the muscle directly, and **magnetic stimulation of the motor cortex** to probe the command signal from the brain.

That combination is important. It lets you separate the two kinds of fatigue instead of lumping them together:

**Peripheral fatigue** is the muscle fibre itself running down — measured through the *twitch force* an electrical pulse produces when the muscle is at rest. You're bypassing the brain entirely and asking the muscle what it can do.

**Central fatigue** is the command signal degrading — measured through *voluntary activation*, the gap between what the muscle can produce when stimulated and what you can produce by trying. If that gap widens, your nervous system is under-delivering.

## What they found — and the number that matters

The headline result confirmed the folklore's *timeline*:

| Session | Fatigue fully resolved by |
|---|---|
| Jump squats | **48 hours** |
| Heavy strength | **72 hours** |
| Maximal sprints | **72 hours** |

So there it is. Heavy lifters and sprinters, the two groups who most often quote "48 hours," are the two groups for whom it was measurably **not enough**. Two days after a hard squat session, they were still not back.

Now the part almost nobody quotes. Here is the authors' own conclusion, verbatim:

> "Strength, jump, and sprint training requiring repeated maximum efforts elicits fatigue that requires up to 72 h to fully resolve, but **this fatigue is not primarily underpinned by decrements in CNS function**."

And the pattern underneath it explains why. Muscle function — the potentiated quadriceps twitch — **was still depressed at 48 hours across all three sessions**. Central function, by contrast, was back much sooner: reduced for 24 hours after jumping and sprinting, 48 hours after heavy strength, and no other central differences emerged at any point.

Read those two timelines side by side. The muscle was still down at 48 hours. The nervous system had mostly recovered before it.

**The fatigue is real. The duration is real. The location is wrong.**

## So how long does central fatigue last?

This is where the second source matters, because it answers the question the training study couldn't. Thomas and colleagues measured at 24, 48, and 72 hours — which is the right window for *training recovery*, but it tells you nothing about what happens in the first hour.

For that, the reference review is Carroll, Taylor and Gandevia in the *Journal of Applied Physiology* (2017). They synthesised the whole recovery literature and found a clear split by contraction type:

```
MAXIMAL contractions (single joint)
  Voluntary activation recovers within ~30 seconds
  MVC force reaches only ~80% by 4-5 minutes
  → central fatigue clears almost immediately; what lingers is muscular

SUSTAINED SUBMAXIMAL contractions
  Voluntary activation may not return to baseline until 20-30 minutes
  Central fatigue contributes ~65% of the strength loss at 5% MVC for 70 min
  Resting twitch does NOT recover appreciably within 20-30 min
  → central fatigue is proportionally LARGER here, and clears in ~half an hour
```

Which produces an answer that fits in one sentence:

**Central fatigue, in the narrow sense of a degraded command signal, resolves in minutes — around 30 minutes for sustained work, well under that after a hard maximal set. What takes 48 to 72 hours is the muscle, and the recovery of the whole system's ability to produce force.**

That's a very different problem than the gym describes. And it's a solvable one.

## Why this matters more after 40

Here the honest position is that the direct evidence gets thinner, and I'm going to say so rather than paper over it.

The 2018 study used **ten trained young male athletes**. That's the population it can speak for. It does not licence a claim about a 47-year-old with a desk job, and I'm not going to invent one.

What can be said without overreaching:

**The practical implication is age-independent even if the physiology isn't.** If most of your post-session fatigue is peripheral, then the tools that fix it are nutritional and muscular — protein, glycogen, sleep, and *not* repeating the same tissue load. Those work at 47 the way they work at 27. The tools the folklore recommends instead — extra rest days, "protecting the CNS," vague nervous-system dread — were aimed at the wrong system all along.

There is a relevant asymmetry for older lifters, and it's documented elsewhere in the recovery literature: eccentric-heavy work (lowering phases, sprint deceleration, running downhill) produces more prolonged peripheral damage and slower repair. So if you're 45-plus and your lift numbers stay flat for a week after a heavy session, **the first suspect is tissue recovery, not your nervous system.**

> ⚠️ **One honest caveat:** the Carroll review notes that the mechanism behind delayed central recovery after prolonged submaximal contractions is, in the authors' own word, **unknown**. So "30 minutes" is a well-supported pattern for typical training, not a law. If you're doing genuinely long grinding sessions, treat the central component as unclear rather than solved.

## What to do with a 72-hour clock

The useful part. If recovery takes up to 72 hours and it's mostly muscular, then:

**1. Stop programming by fear of the CNS.** The most common over-40 mistake is treating a heavy session as a systemic bomb that requires blanket rest. The measured reality is tissue-specific. Heavy squats don't fatigue your pressing; they fatigue your legs.

**2. Separate the tissues, not the calendar.** Twenty years of "wait 48 hours between heavy sessions" collapses when you realise the legs need the rest, not the whole body. A hard lower-body session on Monday doesn't block an upper-body session on Tuesday — the twitch data was measured on the knee extensors specifically.

**3. Feed the actual bottleneck.** Peripheral fatigue is glycogen, metabolites, and contractile damage. That's protein, carbohydrate, sleep, and time — [which the recovery post covers in detail](/posts/sleep-and-recovery/). Nervous-system dread has no nutritional fix because that isn't what broke.

**4. Watch for the pattern the folklore misses.** If your numbers stall for *days* after a hard session, query whether it's the muscle and not the mind. That's the trap in [the "fry your CNS" framing](/posts/does-heavy-lifting-fry-your-cns/) — it makes you stop training a system that was never the limiter.

**5. Use the deload reflex correctly.** The reason [a deload week works](/posts/deload-the-recovery-week-you-keep-skipping/) is that it clears accumulated peripheral fatigue. It is not a nervous-system reboot. Knowing which one it is tells you how much deload is enough.

## The one-line version

**Fatigue after hard training lasts up to 72 hours. Central fatigue — the nervous-system part — is mostly gone in under half an hour. Everything you're actually feeling is the muscle, and the muscle needs food, sleep, and a reason not to be loaded again yet.**

The gym gave you the right rest interval and the wrong reason. Program for the reason.
