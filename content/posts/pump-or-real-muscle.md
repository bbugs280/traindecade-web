# Pump or Real Muscle? How to Tell the Difference

The day I finally *knew* it wasn't just a pump, I wasn't in the gym. I was in the changing room after it, putting my dress shirt back on to head to work — and the fabric tore at the elbow as I slid my arm through.

Not a loose thread. A seam giving way, right around the tricep, exactly where a slim-fit office shirt was never designed to meet an arm that actually trains.

Here's the thing that made the moment stick: a pump would have been gone by then. It fades within an hour or two. But that shirt was going to still not fit tomorrow morning, or the week after. That's the difference, and it's worth getting right — because getting it wrong either makes you chase a feeling that doesn't build anything, or makes you miss the real, quiet evidence that you've actually changed.

## What the pump actually is

The pump is real, and it's not nothing — but it's not growth, either.

What bodybuilders call "the pump," sports scientists call **transient hypertrophy**: a temporary increase in muscle size. During hard sets, your veins get compressed while your arteries keep delivering blood in, so plasma pools inside the muscle. Waste products like lactate draw extra water in behind it. The muscle swells, looks fuller, feels tight. That swelling reaches its peak right after you set the weight down and then, quietly, drains away — most of it gone within a couple of hours.

That's the whole story. It's an aesthetic, temporary effect — not a signal that you've built anything this session.

## Why the pump fools you — and why that matters

The pump feels like progress because, for a brief window, it *looks* like it. Bigger, harder, more defined. It's easy to walk out of the gym convinced you've done something because the mirror in the changing room agrees with you for the next twenty minutes.

The risk isn't enjoying it. The risk is *chasing* it — structuring your training around how full your arms feel, instead of around whether you're actually getting stronger over time.

And here's the part most people never check: your muscle can look meaningfully bigger while a pump is still in your system, even if almost nothing has actually grown. Growth is slow — measured in months and half-kilograms. A pump is fast, and it fades. If you're reading your progress off the mirror right after a workout, you're reading the wrong number.

## The actual test: pump fades, growth doesn't

There's a clean way to tell which one you're looking at.

A pump is **temporary**. If the size you're admiring today is gone tomorrow morning — or gone the moment you've had a shower, eaten, and slept — it was a pump. Real muscle is the sleeve that *still* doesn't fit three weeks from now, on a day you didn't train, at a time of day you didn't just finish a set.

That's what the dress shirt was. It wasn't the post-workout flush. It was the accumulated result of a year of progressive overload, showing up in the one place nobody budgets for it: retail sizing.

The honest rules of thumb:

- **Ask tomorrow, not right after.** Whatever is left when the pump has drained is the real thing.
- **Track the trend, not the day.** One workout can spike your size on the crest of a pump. A month of workouts stacking weight on the bar shows up on the sleeve — and that's the number that matters.
- **Don't get too happy about a pump.** It's temporary by definition. Enjoy it, then go do the work that actually lasts.

## What actually grows muscle (and it isn't the pump)

The research has been consistent on this for a while: the pump is a nice side effect, but mechanical tension — putting a muscle under increasingly heavy load, over and over — is the primary driver of real hypertrophy. That's progressive overload: adding weight, reps, or sets over weeks and months, not chasing a fuller arm in the mirror for an hour.

This is also why scale weight can be a liar during a recomposition. You can be [losing fat and building muscle at the same time](/posts/body-recomposition-men-over-40/), and the scale barely moves — but the same slim-fit shirt that used to skim your arm now won't button over it. The clothes stop being forgiving before the scale admits anything. That's not vanity; it's the physical trace of muscle doing its real job — [a tissue that pays compound interest on your health](/posts/muscle-is-metabolic-armor-not-vanity/), not just a number in the mirror.

## The shirt is the receipt

So no, I don't frame the shirt as a triumphant Hulk moment. I frame it as a net win that came with a receipt.

The pump is the fleeting, pleasant part — and it's fine to enjoy it. But real progress is quieter, slower, and it leaves physical evidence. Sometimes that evidence is a dress shirt you liked, torn at the elbow, that you now have to replace.

Learn to hold both. Chase the tension, not the swelling. And if your shirt stops fitting on a day you didn't train — that's not the pump. That's the progress.

- **Schoenfeld (2010)** — *"The Mechanisms of Muscle Hypertrophy and Their Application to Resistance Training."* Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research. Establishes mechanical tension as the primary driver of hypertrophy, with metabolic stress and cellular swelling as secondary contributors.
- **Morton et al. (2019)** — review on resistance training and muscle growth; mechanical tension from progressive overload is the core stimulus underpinning long-term hypertrophy.
