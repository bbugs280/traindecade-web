Most electrolyte advice is written for a 25-year-old athlete and then handed to a 45-year-old desk worker. It says: drink when you are thirsty, and add electrolytes if you sweat a lot. For the second person, that advice fails — not because it is wrong, but because the signal it depends on has degraded.

Thirst gets less sensitive with age. That is measured, not folk wisdom. An older man can be genuinely down on fluid and not feel like drinking. So "drink when thirsty" quietly becomes "drink less than you need", and no amount of willpower fixes a signal that never arrives.

## What actually changes after 40

Four things, all of them moving in the same direction.

**The thirst threshold rises.** In a double-blind crossover study, healthy men aged 65–78 and men aged 25–32 were given the same hypertonic saline load. The older men felt less thirsty and drank less water, despite a comparable or greater rise in serum sodium. The sensation of thirst is dulled, not the need for fluid.

**The body holds less water to begin with.** Total body water falls about 10–15% with age, mostly from lost lean mass, which means the same absolute fluid loss represents a larger share of the total. A fever or a day of not drinking enough — trivial in a 25-year-old — has more serious consequences in a 55-year-old.

**The kidney gets less precise at both ends.** Glomerular filtration rate falls roughly 6 mL/min per decade. The ability to concentrate urine drops: mean maximum urine osmolality measured about 1,109 mOsm/kg in people under 40 and 882 mOsm/kg in those over 60. So the kidney both wastes water and struggles to dump an excess. It tolerates neither a deficit nor a flood well.

**Sodium regulation gets sloppier.** Basal renin and aldosterone activity decline with age, which impairs the kidney's ability to match urinary sodium loss to sodium intake. The consequence cuts both ways: older adults volume-deplete more easily during illness, and are also more vulnerable to a salt load.

⚠️ **One thing that does not follow from this:** none of it means you should start salting everything. Sodium handling degrading is an argument for *consistency*, not for excess.

## The supplement is mostly not the answer

This is where the industry and the evidence part company.

For a man training under an hour at moderate intensity, in moderate conditions, plain water is sufficient. Harvard's guidance is blunt about it: for the average person there is no evidence electrolyte drinks are a healthier choice than water, and many contain extra calories and sodium that may cause problems for some people. The Australian Institute of Sport and the American College of Sports Medicine land in the same place — electrolytes are for prolonged or intense sweating, hot and humid conditions, or a genuinely heavy sweater.

If you train in a Hong Kong summer, that is your exception. Heat and humidity push sweat rate and sodium loss well past the "plain water is fine" threshold. So does anything over about 90 minutes. Those sessions earn electrolytes. A 45-minute gym session does not.

**What the actual losses look like.** Sodium is the dominant electrolyte in sweat, and individual variation is enormous — roughly 300 mg/L to over 1,800 mg/L. That six-fold spread is why one product cannot be "correct" for everyone, and why any advice giving a single number is guessing. Potassium, magnesium, and calcium are lost in much smaller quantities and are far easier to replace with food.

**Reading a label.** Sodium is the number that matters. Research dietitians generally point to 230 to 690 mg of sodium per serving as a useful range, with the higher end for heavy sweaters and long sessions. A product whose first ingredient is sugar, with 80 mg of sodium, is a soft drink with a mineral claim. Sugar is not automatically bad — a little glucose does help fluid absorption — but it should not be the headline ingredient.

## What to do instead of chasing thirst

The practical fix is to stop using thirst as the trigger and use a schedule plus a colour check.

- **Drink on a schedule, not on thirst.** This is the direct consequence of the blunted signal. Two glasses at fixed points in the day beats waiting for a cue that may not come.
- **Urine colour is the cheap sensor.** Pale straw is the target. Dark yellow is a deficit. This is crude but it does not depend on a sensation you can no longer trust.
- **Weigh yourself around long sessions.** Fluid lost is roughly the weight change. Replace about 1.5 times that loss — the excess is what actually restores fluid balance, not just matches it.
- **Get most electrolytes from food.** A banana, yogurt, leafy greens, potatoes, and salted food cover potassium, calcium, and magnesium without a packet. This is not a compromise; it is the standard recommendation.
- **Keep sodium steady rather than high.** Consistency protects against the volume-depletion side of the age-related change. Loading salt protects against nothing and works against the blood-pressure side.
- **Be more careful, not less, if you are on medication.** Diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and NSAIDs all interact with sodium and potassium handling, and the margin before an effect becomes clinically relevant narrows with age. If you take any of these, this is a conversation for your doctor, not a blog.

## One honest caveat about the framing

Nearly everything above is about adults over 65, because that is where the research money goes. The studies are on 65–78-year-olds, not 45-year-olds. Extrapolating to a man in his forties is reasonable — the direction of every change is the same and the decline starts around 40 — but it is an extrapolation, and the effect size at 45 is smaller than at 70.

**Being 45 does not put you in the danger zone. It puts you in the decade where the signal starts getting quieter.** The reason to fix your habits now is that the correction is trivial at 45 and much harder at 65, when the same deficit lands on a body with less reserve.

That is the shape of most of what matters on a decade scale: not a dramatic cliff, but a slow loss of margin that you either notice early or discover late.

## Sources

- **Hydration Strategies in Older Adults** (review, PMC12300510) — documents reduced thirst sensation with age, a higher osmotic threshold, and the recommendation to increase fluid intake regardless of thirst. Also notes that sodium was more effective at maintaining hydration in younger than in older adults.
- **Stachenfeld NS et al.** — thirst and fluid balance with aging; reduced thirst response to hyperosmotic, hypovolemic, and dehydration stimuli consistently reported in both humans and animal models.
- **Phillips PA, Johnston CI, Gray L (1993)**, *Age and Ageing* 22:26–33 — the dehydration crossover study: after 24 hours of dehydration, older subjects had a greater rise in serum sodium but drank significantly less water than younger subjects.
- **The pathophysiology of fluid and electrolyte balance in the older adult surgical patient**, *Clinical Nutrition* — total body water reduced 10–15% with age; reduced GFR and reduced urine-concentrating ability; blunted thirst; blunted renin-aldosterone response increasing susceptibility to potassium disturbance.
- **Electrolytes in the Aging** (PMC2901254) — cortical glomerulosclerosis and declining GFR and renal plasma flow after age 40; impaired ability to excrete a concentrated or dilute urine, sodium, or potassium.
- **Drug-Induced Electrolyte Disturbances in Older Adults** (PMC13764195) — GFR falls about 6.3 mL/min/1.73 m² per decade; maximum urine osmolality ~1,109 mOsm/kg under 40 versus ~882 mOsm/kg over 60; diuretic users had hyponatremia 20% versus 7.7% of non-users.
- **Sawka MN et al., American College of Sports Medicine position stand: Exercise and Fluid Replacement** — fluid replacement guidance; electrolyte replacement indicated for prolonged exercise and high sweat-sodium losses.
- **Australian Institute of Sport, Sports Supplement Framework — Sports Drinks / Electrolytes** — electrolytes indicated for sessions over 60–90 minutes, hot or humid conditions, or heavy sweaters; plain water adequate below that.
- **Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health, The Nutrition Source: "Do I need electrolyte drinks?"** — for the average person there is no evidence electrolyte drinks are healthier than water; many contain extra calories and high sodium; food sources cover requirements.
- **Sweat sodium concentration range 300–1,800 mg/L** — Baker LB, *Sports Medicine*, on individual variability in sweat electrolyte losses; the basis for rejecting any single-number recommendation.
- **NIH Office of Dietary Supplements** — dietary reference intakes for sodium, potassium, magnesium, and calcium by age band, including the higher magnesium needs for adult males over 31.
