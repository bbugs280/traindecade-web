# Muscle Power: The Longevity Number Your Strength Test Misses

Tell a doctor you want to know your risk of an early death, and the test you'll get almost never moves fast. It measures how *much* you can do — how hard you can squeeze, how heavy you can lift. It never asks the question that a decade of data now says matters more: how *quickly* can you do it?

**Muscle power — force multiplied by speed — beats raw strength as a predictor of mortality, and most men over 40 don't train it at all.** They train the slow half, the heavy half, and leave the speed half to waste away.

## The Number Strength Hides

Strength and power sound like the same thing. They aren't, and the difference is the whole point.

- **Strength** is the maximum force you can produce, at any speed. A heavy deadlift. A hard handgrip squeeze.
- **Power** is force *velocity* — how fast you can recruit that force. A deadlift moved slowly is strength. A sandbag moved explosively is power.

Why does speed matter for how long you live? Because **power declines roughly twice as fast as strength as you age.** You lose the *speed* of force long before you lose the *amount* of it — and the speed is what gets you off a chair, up a flight of stairs, or braced before a fall. Strength asks "can you lift it." Power asks "can you move it *in time*." The second question is the one that keeps people alive and independent.

## The Study That Reframed the Message

The evidence is a 2025 study in *Mayo Clinic Proceedings* — the CLINIMEX Exercise cohort, following **3,888 men and women aged 46 to 75** for a median of **10.8 years**.

Researchers measured both halves — muscle power and muscle strength — and watched who died. The result, adjusted for age, body composition, and multiple health conditions:

- **Relative muscle power** in the lowest group predicted **nearly six times greater mortality risk** in men (hazard ratio **5.88**, P < .001) and nearly seven in women (**6.90**).
- **Relative strength** in the lowest group predicted a hazard ratio of **1.62** in men — with a confidence interval that crosses 1 and a P value of **.11**, meaning it was **not a statistically significant predictor** in this cohort.

Read that again. In the same people, over the same decade, **power was a strong signal and strength barely registered.** The men and women carrying the most muscle power outlived the ones who could only squeeze or lift slowly — even when their raw strength looked fine.

That's the quiet warning for a 40-something man: your strength number can look healthy while your *power* — the thing that actually predicts the next ten years — is quietly eroding.

## How to Train the Speed Half

Power is a trainable quality, and it's not a matter of going maximal-effort heavy every session. The lever is **speed against sub-maximal load**, not more weight.

1. **Move lighter loads fast.** A classic, trainable dose is ~**40–50% of your 1-repetition maximum**, moved as explosively as you safely can. You're not chasing failure; you're chasing *speed of the bar*.
2. **Pick the right movements.** Power lives in big, fast patterns — a **weighted upper-row moved explosively** (the exact movement the study measured), loaded jumps, medicine-ball throws, sled pushes. Not slow, grind-out isolation work.
3. **Two or three hard speed sets, fresh.** Power is a neurological quality. It's best trained at the *start* of a session, when you're fresh — not as a finisher when you're already fatigued.
4. **Don't chase soreness.** If you're wrecked the next day, you trained fatigue, not power. Power sessions feel short and crisp, and they should.

The trap is treating power as "just train heavy." Heavy forces you to move *slow*. What you want is the opposite — sub-maximal and *fast*.

## The Decade-Scale Point

A strength-only plan is training half the signal. It builds the muscle that looks right and lifts heavy, but it lets the thing that predicts your lifespan — the *speed of force* — slip first, quietly, for years.

For a man planning his next decade instead of his next six weeks, the message is simple: **train the number that predicts your longevity, and don't forget the speed half.** Strength is the engine; power is how fast you can use it — and the speed is what you'll miss when you need it most.
