I went to Tokyo for a week. No gym, no plan, no training log. Just walking and eating and being on holiday.

The first session back, the bench moved differently. Not a heroic jump — one level up. The kind of jump that normally takes two or three months of grinding to earn. Every lifter waits for that moment, and most of us never learn what caused it, so we can't repeat it.

So this post is me trying to answer my own question. What actually happened in that week?

## What most people will tell you (and why it's half true)

The standard answer is "deload rebound." You backed off, fatigue cleared, and the strength you'd already built finally became visible.

That's a real mechanism. Hard training builds adaptation and fatigue at the same time. Fatigue shows up first and hides the adaptation underneath it. Remove the fatigue and the strength is *revealed*.

But the word "deload" has been quietly stretched to cover two different things, and the difference decides whether you come back stronger or flat.

| | A deload week | A week off on holiday |
|---|---|---|
| Still in the gym? | Yes | No |
| Load | Cut | Zero |
| Frequency | Kept | Zero |
| Daily movement | Normal | Often much higher |
| Sleep | Often unchanged | Often different |
| Fatigue | Falls | Falls |
| **Practice of the movement** | **Kept** | **Lost** |

They both clear fatigue. They do not do the same thing to your skill.

## The trial where the week off went badly

Here's where the honest version starts, and it's uncomfortable for anyone selling the rebound as a law.

A 2024 trial in *PeerJ* (Coleman and colleagues) tested a one-week break directly. Thirty-nine resistance-trained young adults ran a 9-week program at high volume — about 90 weekly sets, sets taken to volitional failure. Half took a complete week off at the midpoint. Half trained straight through.

The group that took the week off did **not** get stronger. They went slightly backwards on lower-body strength. Their muscle growth was a wash. And the subjective result is the one that matters for this post: they reported *increased* soreness and *decreased* motivation, with several describing themselves as lethargic and "out of practice" on return.

That trial is the strongest evidence against the version of this story where a week off is a free gift. It's also the second half of an argument I made in [After the Deload](/posts/after-the-deload-rebound/): the rebound was never automatic, and the way you come back decides whether any of it shows up.

So why didn't that happen to me?

## The difference is not the rest — it's the *kind* of rest

Look again at the table. The Coleman subjects stopped training and changed nothing else. They kept their desk, their commute, their normal daily step count. They removed the stimulus and kept the sedentary baseline.

A holiday is not that. A week in Tokyo is usually 15,000–20,000 steps a day, no desk, no commute, and a very different sleep pattern. That is a week of **low-intensity, high-volume movement** — which is not detraining. It's the opposite of sitting still.

That reframe matters, because it changes what you're supposed to do. If the benefit came from *rest*, the lesson is "rest more." If it came partly from *movement plus rest*, the lesson is "walk on your holiday" — which is a very different instruction, and a much easier one to actually follow.

⚠️ **I can't prove which one it was in my case.** I didn't measure my steps in Tokyo. I'm telling you the honest version: the mechanism most likely to explain my bench is reduced training fatigue combined with unusually high easy movement, not rest alone. That's a hypothesis with good support behind each half, not a measured result about me.

## What the evidence does say about stopping for a week

Now the reassuring part, because the "you'll lose your gains" fear is the reason most lifters never take the week.

**You do not lose measurable strength in a week.** A 2018 study in the *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research* (Pritchard and colleagues) put eight resistance-trained men through two 4-week strength blocks, then had them stop for either 3.5 or 5.5 days. Isometric bench press peak force and jump height both went **up**, significantly, by the end of the break. The authors attributed it to *decreased neuromuscular fatigue* — a more efficient nervous system expressing the strength that was already there.

Note what that is: the gains showed up in a *force* measure, and the explanation was neural, not muscular. Nobody grew a pectoral in five days.

**And short breaks don't cost you long-term progress.** Ogasawara and colleagues ran a series of bench-press trials on this. In the 2011 study, a 3-week detraining period produced **no significant decrease** in 1RM or in MRI-measured triceps and pectoral size, and the retraining gains afterwards matched the initial training gains. In the 2013 follow-up, lifters who trained in cycles separated by 3-week breaks finished 24 weeks with the **same** muscle and strength gains as the group that never stopped — and gained *faster* in the second retraining cycle.

That last detail is worth sitting with. Periodic breaks didn't just avoid hurting progress. In that trial they made some of it come faster.

## Why the same week leaves some lifters flat

If a week off can help, why did Coleman's subjects come back worse? Three conditions separate the two outcomes, and they're all things you can control.

**1. Movement doesn't stop when training does.** The lifters who came back flat removed the training stimulus and replaced it with nothing. A holiday where you walk 15,000 steps a day is not nothing. A holiday where you lie by a pool for seven days, followed by a desk, is closer to the Coleman protocol.

**2. Sleep is part of the week.** This is the part I got for free in Tokyo and almost nobody plans for. The evidence here is specific and it matters for a bench press more than for a curl. A 2018 systematic review (Knowles and colleagues, 17 studies) found that consecutive nights of restricted sleep reduced force output in **multi-joint** movements — bench press, leg press — but *not* single-joint ones. The researchers' conclusion is the sharpest line in this whole post: inadequate sleep impairs maximal strength in compound movements *when performed without something to boost motivation*.

A holiday tends to deliver both halves: more sleep, and less accumulated fatigue to fight through. A week of short sleep at work delivers neither.

**3. The re-entry is where it's usually lost.** You don't come back to a blank slate. You come back to a body that has been out of the pattern for seven days. If you walk in and immediately load your old working weights, you spend the first session re-learning the groove under maximum load. That's how a good week produces a bad session, and how lifters conclude the break cost them something.

## The honest limit — say it plainly

Everything above is about strength *expression*. Not one of these studies shows that taking a week off builds new muscle.

```text
WHAT A WEEK OFF CAN DO
  ✓ clear accumulated fatigue
  ✓ let stored strength become visible in a test
  ✓ improve force output via the nervous system
  ✓ cost you nothing measurable in strength or size
  ✓ not slow your long-term progress over 24 weeks

WHAT A WEEK OFF CANNOT DO
  ✗ build muscle in seven days
  ✗ override a bad re-entry
  ✗ rescue a programme that isn't working
  ✗ replace the training that produces the adaptation
```

The adaptation was built in the months before the holiday. The holiday only decided whether you got to *see* it.

That's why the story feels magical and the mechanism is mundane. You did the work for months. A week of no fatigue and good sleep finally let the bar tell you about it.

## What to actually do with this

If you're taking a week off — a holiday, a work trip, an enforced break — the evidence points at four things. None of them are a supplement.

**1. Keep moving, easily.** The point of a holiday is not to become a training session. Walking, swimming, whatever the place offers. You're trying to avoid the Coleman condition of "stimulus removed, everything else identical."

**2. Protect sleep more than you protect training.** Sleep loss hits compound lifts specifically, which is where your PRs live. A week of good sleep is a training intervention; a week of late nights to "make the most of the trip" is not. If you want the full case for why sleep is the input most training programmes quietly under-count, it's in [sleep and recovery](/posts/sleep-and-recovery/).

**3. Come back at about 85%, for one session.** Rebuild the pattern before you chase the number. Then let the load find you — if the bar moves well at 85%, it will tell you so, and you'll have a real session rather than a hopeful one.

**4. Take the break on purpose, not by accident.** This is the actual lesson of my week. Tokyo wasn't a planned deload. It was a holiday that happened to be shaped like one, and I got the rebound as a side effect. A break you plan has the movement and the sleep built in. A break you don't plan is a coin toss — and Coleman's trial is what the losing side looks like.

## The bottom line

A week off didn't make me stronger. It let me find out that I already was.

The fatigue of a hard training block is real, and it hides the thing you've been working for. Remove it, alongside a week of easy movement and decent sleep, and the bar tells you the truth about what you built.

The mistake is expecting that from *any* week off. The lifters who came back flat weren't unlucky. They took the stimulus away and replaced it with nothing, then returned at full load.

The week off is not the gift. It's the moment the gift becomes visible. If you want the other half — how to structure the weeks *on* so the fatigue you're clearing is fatigue worth having — start with [the deload you keep skipping](/posts/deload-the-recovery-week-you-keep-skipping/).

PMID 38274324. 39 resistance-trained adults, 9-week high-volume programme, complete 1-week cessation at midpoint vs continuous training. - **Pritchard HJ, Barnes MJ, Stewart RJC, Keogh JWL, McGuigan MR (2018)** — "Short-Term Training Cessation as a Method of Tapering to Improve Maximal Strength," *J Strength Cond Res* 32(2):458–465. Eight resistance-trained men; two 4-week strength blocks followed by either ~3.5 or ~5.5 days of training cessation. Countermovement jump height and isometric bench press peak force increased significantly from pre-training to post-cessation (effect sizes 0.30 and 0.21). Authors attribute the improvement to decreased neuromuscular fatigue.
- **Ogasawara R, Yasuda T, Sakamaki M, Ozaki H, Abe T (2011)** — "Effects of periodic and continued resistance training on muscle CSA and strength in previously untrained men," *Clin Physiol Funct Imaging* 31(5):399–404. Bench press training with a 3-week detraining period: no significant decrease in 1RM or MRI-measured triceps brachii and pectoralis major cross-sectional area; retraining gains matched initial gains; 15-week outcomes equivalent to continuous training.
- **Ogasawara R, Yasuda T, Ishii N, Abe T (2013)** — "Comparison of muscle hypertrophy following 6-month of continuous and periodic strength training," *Eur J Appl Physiol* 113(4):975–985. 14 young men, 24 weeks, continuous vs three 6-week cycles separated by 3-week detraining periods. Overall muscle CSA and 1RM gains were **similar** between groups; the periodic group's gains in the second retraining cycle were significantly higher than the continuous group's over the corresponding period. Detraining caused small 1RM decreases (−2.0% and −3.3%) while maximal voluntary contraction was unchanged.
- **Knowles OE, Drinkwater EJ, Urwin CS, Lamon S, Aisbett B (2018)** — "Inadequate sleep and muscle strength: Implications for resistance training," *J Sci Med Sport* 21(9):959–968. Systematic review, 17 studies, rated moderate to weak quality. Sleep deprivation had little effect on strength; consecutive nights of sleep restriction reduced force output in multi-joint but not single-joint movements. Conclusion: inadequate sleep impairs maximal muscle strength in compound movements when performed without interventions designed to increase motivation.

⚠️ **Population note:** the Coleman, Pritchard and Ogasawara cohorts were young and, in two cases, small (n=8 and n=9 per group). None tested men over 40. Directions of effect should not be read as measured magnitudes for a 45-year-old. The sleep review is the one source here that speaks directly to a mechanism that worsens with age, because sleep quality is more fragile over 40.
