If you wear a watch or a band, you know the picture: it's eleven at night, you're two thousand steps short, so you walk laps around your building. The watch sets off fireworks and you feel like you've done your duty for the day.

That number — 10,000 — you've assumed is a scientific standard. It isn't. It was a slogan invented in 1965 by a Japanese clock company to sell pedometers, and at the time there was no research behind it at all. The science only got bolted on decades later.

Nothing on this page is selling you a pedometer. This is just the actual large-scale research, walked through properly, with every study named and linked so you can read it and decide for yourself whether we've got it right.

## The short version

If you want the answer before the detail: **for men over 40, the number that carries real meaning sits around 7,000 steps a day, not 10,000.** And here's the counter-intuitive part — **volume matters, walking speed barely does.** Once studies control for total step count, the extra benefit from walking faster essentially disappears.

Here's why, and the one habit you could change tonight.

## Where the number came from

In 1963, Japan was preparing to host the Tokyo Olympics, and a worry was building: daily life was getting more convenient — elevators, cars, escalators — and people were moving less. A Tokyo clinic director named Iwao Ohya raised this with an engineer at the clock and instrument company Yamasa Tokei Keiki, Jiro Kato.

In 1965, Yamasa released the world's first consumer pedometer, called the **manpo-kei** — literally "10,000-step meter." The marketing line was "10,000 steps a day for your health."

As for **why 10,000**? The company couldn't say. Both men have since died, and a Yamasa spokesperson apologised for the company's lack of information about where the figure came from. The Japanese character for 10,000 (万) looks vaguely like a person walking, if you squint. Beyond that, 10,000 simply sounded more impressive than 7,000, and people like round numbers.

**The actual science came fifteen years later.** In the late 1970s, Yoshiro Hatano at Tokyo Gakugei University began studying the number with pedometers and treadmills. So the order was: **slogan first, research second** — not the other way round.

That matters, because it explains why the number spent decades not matching the evidence.

## What the research actually says

### 1. The threshold is lower than 10,000, and it depends on age

**Paluch et al. 2022, The Lancet Public Health** — this is the most important study on the question. Fifteen international cohorts, 47,471 adults, 3,013 deaths, median follow-up 7.1 years. It wasn't questionnaire-based: each study team reprocessed its raw participant data to a common protocol.

| Steps per day | All-cause mortality vs lowest group |
|---|---|
| 3,553 (lowest) | baseline |
| 5,801 | **−40%** (HR 0.60) |
| 7,842 | **−45%** (HR 0.55) |
| 10,901 | **−53%** (HR 0.47) |

But the shape of the curve is the real finding. Using restricted cubic splines, the risk reduction is **diminishing**, and **the inflection point moves with age**:

- **Over 60:** benefits level off around **6,000–8,000 steps**
- **Under 60:** benefits level off around **8,000–10,000 steps**

So for someone in their forties or fifties, **7,000 to 10,000 is the zone** — and most of the gain is banked in the earlier part of it.

### 2. Walking faster adds almost nothing

The same study also looked at **stepping rate** (steps per minute). This is its most valuable contribution.

Once researchers adjusted for **total steps per day**, the extra effect of pace collapsed. The initially significant "30 minutes a day of brisk walking" association attenuated to HR 0.67, 60 minutes to 0.67, and the groups walking at 40+ steps per minute or 100+ steps per minute were **not statistically significant**.

In plain language: **how fast you walk isn't the point. How much you walk is.** That directly undercuts the idea that only a dedicated brisk-walk session counts as exercise.

### 3. 10,000 isn't wrong, but its marginal value is thin

**The Lancet Public Health 2025** (57 studies, 35 cohorts) makes this clearest. The inflection points land between **5,000 and 7,000 steps**:

- Against 2,000 steps a day, **7,000 steps** is associated with **47% lower all-cause mortality** (HR 0.53), 25% lower cardiovascular disease incidence, plus improvements in dementia, falls and depression
- Against 7,000 steps, **10,000 steps** adds only about **10%** more all-cause mortality reduction — and shows **no statistical difference** for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes or falls

And even very low counts matter: **4,000 steps versus 2,000** is already 36% lower all-cause mortality.

So 10,000 isn't wrong. It's just "fine," not "required." The real message is that **every thousand steps counts, especially the first few thousand.**

## Why this habit outlasts the others

Past 40 you notice something: **the exercise you put in the calendar is the first thing to get cancelled.**

A trip, a late meeting, a sick kid, a back that seizes up — any one of them deletes "gym tonight." That's why so many training plans don't survive three months.

**Daily steps don't get cancelled**, because they aren't a separate activity you have to schedule. They're the sum of movement you were already doing — commuting, walking between meetings, going for coffee, taking the stairs. They don't reset to zero because today was busy.

This is why NEAT (non-exercise activity thermogenesis) matters disproportionately after 40: **it's the form of activity least likely to be interrupted.** It survives injury, travel and collapsed motivation.

For someone carrying a back problem and a shoulder problem — as I am — that's the decisive property: low impact, chunkable, stoppable at any point, and no recovery cost.

## Things you could change tonight

You don't need a band. Your phone is already counting.

**1. Look at your baseline before setting a target.**
Open your phone's health app and check your two-week average. Most desk workers land between 4,000 and 7,000. You need the starting point before you know how much to add.

**2. Add one anchored movement, not "more walking."**
The reliable pattern is to attach it to something you already do:
- Stand up and walk during phone calls
- Get off one stop early
- Walk 15 minutes after a meal (roughly 1,500–2,000 steps)
- Take the stairs instead of the lift, until you've had enough

**3. Set the target at 7,000, not 10,000.**
On the current evidence, 7,000 is a target you'll hit; 10,000 is one you'll abandon. And 7,000 already captures most of the benefit.

**4. Ignore speed.**
Unless you're doing separate aerobic training (see Zone 2 below), don't worry about walking "fast enough." Just accumulate.

**5. Measure it in decades, not weeks.**
Same position as the rest of this site: six-week challenges fail, ten-year habits don't.

## Common objections

**"Walking doesn't count as exercise."**
If you mean "it won't raise VO2max," that's partly fair — walking genuinely won't push aerobic capacity the way Zone 2 training does. If you mean "it doesn't affect lifespan," that's wrong. Every study cited here measured all-cause mortality.

**"You have to do it all at once."**
Every one of these studies measured **daily totals**, not continuous bouts. Accumulated and continuous are not treated separately in the data.

**"I need a weighted vest to make it count."**
That's the next topic, and the answer is more complicated than you'd expect — the point isn't how much you carry, but **how** you carry it. Wear-only loading showed null results in the research; task-based progressive loading is what moved bone density. That's the next post.

## The takeaway

- **10,000 steps was a 1965 pedometer slogan**, not a research finding. The science came fifteen years later.
- **Paluch 2022 (Lancet, 15 cohorts, 47,471 people):** all-cause mortality falls with step count, but the curve diminishes — inflecting at 6,000–8,000 for over-60s and 8,000–10,000 for under-60s.
- **Once total steps are controlled for, walking pace adds almost nothing** — the single most counter-intuitive finding here.
- **Lancet 2025:** 7,000 steps versus 2,000 = 47% lower all-cause mortality; going to 10,000 adds only ~10% more, with no statistical difference for other outcomes.
- **Even 4,000 steps versus 2,000 is already 36% lower.** The first few thousand are the most valuable.
- **Set the target at 7,000, not 10,000.** One you'll reach, one you'll quit.
- **Ignore speed.** Accumulate volume.
